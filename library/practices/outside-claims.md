# Practice — Outside claims (when a stranger tells you how to build)

> **The problem this kills: adopting on confidence instead of evidence.** Your feed is full of *"you
> must do X"* — a Reddit thread, a conference talk, a blog post with a number in the headline. Most of
> it is real advice from real practitioners, formed in a context that is not yours. Adopted
> unexamined, it is how a solo founder with no users ends up running microservices, writing a test
> pyramid for an app nobody has opened, or rebuilding on the framework that was loud that month.
>
> The cost is not the idea. It is the **weeks**, and the fact that added ceremony always lands on the
> person who can least afford it.

This is a two-minute discipline, not a research project. Run it when a claim is about to change what
you do.

## 1. Verify who actually said it — *before* you grade it

**Do this first, because everything downstream inherits it.** A claim's authority is half its weight,
and it is the half nobody checks. If the attribution is wrong, the grade you give it is a grade on
something that does not exist.

The failure modes are specific, and each one is real:

- **Half-right.** The person named said something *adjacent* — a similar claim, a weaker version, or
  the opposite in a later revision. Being famous for an idea is not the same as having said this.
- **The wrong half of the number.** A real study, a real figure, describing something else — an
  *injection* rate quoted as an *attack* rate, a sample quoted as a population.
- **A false clause bolted to a true citation.** The first half checks out, so the second half rides
  along unexamined. Verify every clause in a sentence, not the one with the name attached.
- **A publisher that does not exist.** Search summaries invent plausible institutions and re-date real
  findings. **Verify the publisher exists before verifying what it said,** and treat the DATE as part
  of the attribution.
- **Retracted, and still circulating.** The best-known version of an idea is sometimes the one its
  author later took back. A famous quote is evidence of fame, not of currency.
- **A quote whose meaning depends on which line a marker attaches to.** Footnotes, asterisks, table
  cells. A summary is a *hypothesis* about a document; it is least trustworthy exactly here.

**Open the primary.** Not the summary, not the blog post citing it — the thing itself. If you cannot
open it, that is a finding: say the claim is unverified rather than grading it as though you had.
Cite the document you actually opened, and if it differs from the one everybody cites, say both.

## 2. Grade what kind of thing it is

Three rungs, and most claims are the first:

- **Anecdote** — it worked for them, once, in their context. Legitimate and weak.
- **Argument** — reasoning from principles, no measurement. As good as the reasoning.
- **Measured** — somebody counted. Now ask **whose context**: team size, stage, stack, users. A
  practice measured at 200 engineers is not evidence about you.

## 3. Default to NO — and say that you are

Skepticism toward a stranger's claim is a *legitimate default*, because the asymmetry is real: a bad
adopt costs weeks, and a missed good idea costs one more encounter with it later. But **a held bias is
not neutral truth.** Say which one you are exercising, out loud, or it hardens into "obviously not."

## 4. Name who it serves, and what it costs *you*

Every practice serves someone. Ask who this one was designed for, and whether that is you yet. Then
price it honestly: **added ceremony is a cost**, paid every time, by you.

## 5. Write the re-open condition

A `no` is a decision, not a verdict on the idea forever. Name what would change your mind — *"when we
have a second engineer"*, *"when a paying customer asks"* — and it stops being something you
re-litigate every time it comes round again. A `no` with no re-open condition gets re-argued monthly.

## What this is NOT

- **Not a reason to ignore expertise.** The point is to adopt *deliberately*, not to adopt less. The
  most common failure is not gullibility; it is adopting five things and finishing none.
- **Not a research process.** If a claim needs a literature review to settle, the honest answer is
  usually *"not now"* — and that is a complete answer.
- **Not for your own proven patterns.** Something you built, that worked, that repeated, has earned
  trust this practice is about *withholding*. That is `/practice` and `/boss-learn`.
