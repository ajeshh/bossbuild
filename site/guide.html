▸the one idea

When to use BOSS, and when to just talk to Claude

BOSS doesn't sit between you and Claude. You build exactly as you do now. The split is simple:

just ask Claude

“How do I build this?”

No skill, no ceremony. That's most of your day, and BOSS stays out of it.

a BOSS verb

“Should I build this? Is it working? What did I decide?”

The seams between building, where things get lost.

While you're heads-down, BOSS does almost nothing. That's the design, not a gap.

Quickstart, in full: get the idea out of your head

Capture freely with /idea. Pressure-test one with /canvas, filled a few cells at a time rather than all at once. You never pick a framework: you answer questions, and the frame is a view you switch. The same answers render as the Humane Product Canvas (the default), a Lean Canvas, or a Business Model Canvas; /canvas --frame lean re-renders what you've already said rather than interviewing you again, and a cell you haven't answered shows as _(not yet)_ in every frame instead of quietly disappearing to make a view look complete. A fourth frame, /canvas --frame onepager, folds the same answers into the smallest thing you can hand to somebody outside, and it refuses to render at all until at least one piece of evidence is on file.

Commercial prompts are folded into every cell (who exactly has the problem, what they use instead, who pays and why). And two questions ride along in all three frames, not just the humane one: who this could harm or exclude, and the principles you'll hold when holding them costs you something. Un-defaulting the framework was never meant to make those optional.

Then find out if it's real, which is the half that doesn't happen in the editor. /interview preps one 15-minute conversation under Mom-Test discipline (past behaviour, their life, no pitching) and debriefs your notes into graded records afterwards. /pretotype builds the fake door: a real page you can put in front of real people, published in one turn, so demand gets tested while it still costs an afternoon. Both live here, at the first rung, because testing whether anyone wants the thing is not an advanced move, and holding it back until MVP had it arriving after the build it was meant to prevent.

Work out who it's for with /persona: your target user as a voice you can actually consult, both to decide what to build and to react to what you built. It carries a visible ledger of how much of that user is real evidence and how much is still a guess, and it starts at all guess. It's a way to sharpen the questions you take to a real person, never a way to skip them. Record what you learn with /evidence, and what you chose with /decide. A project that lives here forever is a legitimate project.

MVP, in full: build the first working version

Adds the spec discipline (/spec, with validated-learning and evals fields) and the smoke gate, the first rung where "is it alive?" becomes a gate rather than a habit. Design tokens get scaffolded here too, the moment there's real UI, so style never gets locked into code, and the designer arrives with them — one designer for both halves, what it looks like and what it does (/design-review, /ux-check).

It's also where the questions start pointing outward. /comp-eval goes and finds out who else is solving this (features, pricing, and the honest “why they might win”), then keeps it as a living set you add names to, rather than a slide you drew once. /roadmap weighs what to build next into a short bet-list with a mandatory no-list, because a roadmap that only says yes isn't a decision.

A second set of verbs whose moment only arrives once something is live: retention, pricing, measurement, health, the first dollar. They install here but stay folded away until you've actually shipped a feature, so a founder with one idea isn't read a menu of verbs about pricing. And a project can end at any rung. /sunset exists because quitting well is a skill, and a tool that only knows how to grow things is lying to you about how this usually goes.

V1, in full: make it shippable

V1 hires nobody. It's the one rung that adds no agents at all, and that's deliberate. By now you have the team; what you don't have is a way to see what you've already made. So it adds two views. /design-library generates a gallery of your components, every variant, all five states, from your code, plus a reuse index the agent reads before writing a new one. /board sequences work across features instead of within one.

▸with a cofounder

Building with someone else

AI made each of you faster, which, left alone, makes a two-person team drift apart. You each work in your own AI session, ship in parallel, and quietly stop deciding things together. The uncomfortable part: that erosion is invisible from the inside. Teams that have already drifted still rate their teamwork as fine.

boss team add @their-handle lights up a shared place to decide (/decide), a shared craft commons (/practice), and mentor-cofounder, the one mentor that coaches the relationship. All of it is dormant when you're solo. Keyed to GitHub handles: no accounts, no seats, no server, no hierarchy.

shared — commits with the repo
  • your ideas and the canvas
  • your decisions and practices
  • the devlog
  • the venture brain's read on the project

Pushing your repo backs up your thinking and keeps your cofounder in the loop: same action, both jobs.

private — never leaves your machine
  • the conscience's relationship notes
  • what it said to you
  • what you overrode, what it is holding back

Your cofounder never sees how often you have been nudged.

What the conscience watches, and what it won't

mentor-cofounder coaches dividing work across different skill sets, decision rights, and the hard conversations about roles, pace and equity. It serves the partnership as a unit and never takes a side.

If real work has happened and you have never recorded a single decision together, the conscience says so once: "you've been building a while and haven't decided anything jointly; are you two actually deciding this together, or in parallel?" It notices that through the artifacts, an empty decision log, and never by asking "how's your teamwork?", because self-report is precisely what is blind to this drift. It is deliberately conservative, because a quiet decision log can just mean you decide on a call; if that is you, boss conscience mute coordination and it stops. It surfaces the seam. It never says whose fault it is, and it never scores your equity.

▸worth remembering

Two things

  • boss status is the one command to remember. Come back after a week and it tells you where you are and what's next: you are here, the ladder, the one thing to pick up, and what you've actually learned so far. Its companion is boss recap, which reads the last week back out of your own records (landed / learned / decided / in flight) and says a section is empty rather than padding it. boss recap --md if you need to paste it to someone.
  • The conscience is always overridable. boss conscience pause --for 8h for a sprint, or mute <moment> to turn down one nudge while the rest keep speaking. It never blocks.

The full guide · and once BOSS is installed, boss help --html writes the whole thing as a page for your project: every skill you actually have, the rungs you have not climbed folded but searchable, and nothing you would have to keep up to date.

make it real.
$npm install -g oyeboss
$brew install ajeshh/boss/oyeboss

Either puts boss on your PATH. Needs Node 18+ and Claude Code, in the terminal, VS Code, Cursor or JetBrains, on macOS, Linux or Windows; BOSS runs wherever Claude Code does.

The source is on GitHub and the package is on npm. MIT licensed, zero dependencies, no account.

Built by Ajesh Shah in Oakland, California; the why is here. Ajesh is on LinkedIn and GitHub.

Every byte stays on your machine. The only network call BOSS makes is when you type boss update: a GET to the public npm registry asking for a version number. Nothing is sent about you, your code, or your ideas. No analytics, no telemetry, no phone-home; the check never runs unless you ask for it. It’s ~30 lines in src/update.js and you can read them.

Where this actually is: v0.326.0, built in the open and used daily by its author. Very few other people have used it yet. BOSS is its own first project: it runs its own modes, files its own ideas, and its conscience nudges the person building it. If that sounds like a reason to wait, that’s a fair read.
