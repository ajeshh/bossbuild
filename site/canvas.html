The Humane Product Canvas

Two questions the other canvases don't ask.

A one-page canvas for pressure-testing an idea — who it's for, what it promises, how it sustains itself. It covers what the Lean Canvas and the Business Model Canvas cover, and then asks two more: who this could harm or exclude, and the principles you'll hold when holding them costs you something.

Free, and nothing to install. It's a page and a set of questions — a napkin works. Take the template, print it, fork it, teach from it. Download the template →

▸the two

Why two more cells

Every canvas asks who the customer is and how the money works. None of the standard ones asks who gets hurt, and none asks what you won't trade away. Those get decided anyway — just later, under pressure, by whoever is in the room. A cell is the cheapest possible way to decide them early.

Risks & Harms What could unintentionally go wrong, and who might be harmed or excluded? Name the worst-served person, not an abstraction. Who is this worst for? What happens to someone who uses it at 3am, or in crisis, or without the context everyone else has? And the question people skip: what's the harm if it works exactly as designed?
Principles The non-negotiables you'll hold even when holding them costs you something. That last clause is the whole cell — a principle you've never paid for isn't one yet, it's a preference.

Neither is a compliance exercise, and neither is at the end. They sit in the canvas with everything else, because that's where they change decisions.

▸the canvas

Eleven cells, three sections

Fill a few at a time. It's a snapshot, not a blueprint — you're meant to come back to it.

1 · Human Foundation

People — who exactly has the painful problem, and who feels it worst today? How many are there, and how do you know? Count bottom-up: a list, a forum, a job title you can filter on. "$50B market" is not an answer; "about 4,000 of these exist and I can name where they gather" is.
Problem — what real human tension is this? What do they use today instead — including the spreadsheet, the agency, the intern, and doing nothing? For each real one, why might they win?
Promises — the sharp version: "we help X do Y without Z."

2 · Product Expression

Story — how does this show up in someone's life, and what changed that makes it newly possible?
Modes of Engagement — how do people interact with it, and does it respect their time, attention, autonomy and data? What kind of relationship is this — self-serve, hands-on, community? Name it, because it sets what you'll owe them later.
Business Model — how will you sustain this without compromising the promise? (see below — this one has two branches)
Plus three that stay blank until they apply: cost structure, what it takes to deliver, key partnerships.

3 · Stewardship

Metrics — real pull (activation, retention, willingness to pay), and the regenerative ones weighted as seriously: what people learn or gain, and what makes the venture more resilient rather than more extractive.
Risks & Harms and Principles — the two above.
Build or buy? — for tool-shaped ideas only. The humane answer is sometimes don't build it yourself.

And then the one that matters most

The riskiest assumption — if this is wrong, none of the rest works. Name it, name the smallest experiment that would prove or disprove it this week, and decide in advance what result would change the plan. A canvas without this is a description; with it, it's a test.

▸not everyone is selling

The business-model cell has two branches

Open source, Creative Commons, a research tool, something you're building for friends or for fun — the cell still applies, because "how will you sustain this" was never a question about money. Only the follow-ups were.

So if it won't earn, answer the other branch: what keeps this alive (your hours are the budget — how many a week, and for how long?), who else could carry it, what happens when you get bored, busy, or hit by a bus, and what would make you stop.

None of that is softer than pricing. Most open-source projects die of maintainer exhaustion, not of a missing business model — and "indefinitely" is the answer that ends projects.

▸how to fill it
A few cells at a time Not a form to complete in one sitting. Fill what you know, come back when you learn something. Date it and keep the old versions — the change is the interesting part.
Blanks are honest signal Leave a cell empty rather than filling it with something plausible. An invented answer is worse than a blank, because a blank tells you where to go next and a guess tells you nothing while looking like progress.
It never replaces talking to people The canvas sharpens the questions you take to a real person. It cannot answer them. If a cell keeps feeling slippery, that's usually the cell to go ask about.

▸the tool behind it

Or let something ask you the questions

This canvas is the paper version of one thing BOSS does. In BOSS it's /canvas — the same questions, asked a few at a time as the work earns them, kept alongside your evidence and your decisions, and rendered as a Lean Canvas or a Business Model Canvas when the person reading expects one. It also notices when your canvas has fallen behind the product you actually shipped.

You don't need any of that to use the canvas. The paper version is the whole framework, given away on purpose. The tool is for when you want it to keep asking.

What BOSS is →

make it real.
$npx oyeboss new my-app

Source on GitHub · npm · MIT licensed · zero dependencies · no account.

Every byte stays on your machine. The only network call BOSS makes is when you type boss update — a GET to the public npm registry asking for a version number. Nothing is sent about you, your code, or your ideas. No analytics, no telemetry, no phone-home; the check never runs unless you ask for it.

Where this actually is: v0.198.0, built in the open and used daily by its author. Very few other people have used it yet. BOSS is its own first project — it runs its own modes, files its own ideas, and its conscience nudges the person building it.
