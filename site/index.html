B.O.S.S.

Build Out Solid Stuff

Build it like you have a team. Because now you do.

BOSS is a startup incubator that runs inside Claude Code. It puts the structure of a company around your code — how to decide what to build, how to find out if anyone wants it, when to ship, and when the discipline should get out of your way — and it speaks up when you're drifting. Free, MIT, zero dependencies, runs entirely on your machine.

▸the problem

Building got cheap. Being wrong didn't.

Everyone can build now. Almost nobody can tell a real thing from a convincing demo. AI made a polished prototype nearly free, which created a new trap: an impressive demo with no proven pain, no workflow fit and nobody who would miss it — sitting in a folder next to two more you didn't finish.

Cheap AI lowers the cost of building, not the cost of being wrong. So the cheaper building gets, the more the loop around it matters — and the loop is the part nobody handed you.

BOSS helps you build faster without fooling yourself.

Not a guarantee you'll win — a way to decide faster, including quitting faster. That's the honest claim, and it's the only one on this page.

▸what you get

Two weeks in, you have four things you didn't have

Not features — files. Each one is a real artifact you can open, hand to someone, or argue with.

  • An idea that shrank to something you can test on Sunday /idea

    You arrive with "build me a scheduling app." You leave with one week, one cleaner, one text sent by hand — the smallest version that would actually prove it. That line is the whole job.

  • A signal from a real person, graded honestly /interview /evidence

    A fixed three-rung ladder — stated-pain → observed-behavior → commitment — and BOSS grades what you bring it, not what you hoped for. Somebody saying "great idea" lands on rung one and stays there. The conscience gets quieter when real commitments exist, which is the only reward on offer.

  • A decision with a cheap way to find out it was wrong /decide

    Context, the call, why — and a falsifier: what would disprove this, and by when. Lighter than an RFC, denser than a commit message, and readable by a cofounder who wasn't in the room.

  • One page you can hand to someone outside /canvas --frame onepager

    The same answers you already gave, laid out for somebody who has four minutes. It refuses to render if you have no evidence on file — because a one-pager backed by nothing is the most convincing wrong thing you can make.

Before any of it, /pretotype builds a fake door — a real page you can put in front of real people — so you can find out whether anyone wants the thing while it still costs an afternoon. That's available on day one, not after you've built something.

▸is this you?

Three shapes this actually fits

Pick the one that sounds like your week.

An idea you can't stop thinking about

You've been building with AI for a few months. You shipped one small thing, there are two more in ~/projects/ you didn't finish, and a folder of ideas you never started. This one feels different — but so did those.

What BOSS does: helps you find the smallest version that would actually prove it — something you can put in front of one real person this week, before you spend a month building the version in your head. If the idea isn't real, you find that out in days instead of at launch.

Day one is 4 agents and 17 skills, and four files that are yours to read. No specs, no tests, no design system — those arrive later, if the thing works. → boss new

An app that's live, with people paying for it

It works. Now an AI feature is going in, and the hard question isn't how to build it — it's whether it's worth building at all, and what it will cost per customer once real people are using it.

What BOSS does: reads what's already there and tells you where you stand, then helps with the decision rather than the typing. Your code is not touched. Anything you wrote yourself stays yours — it tells you what it added, and later updates skip your files.

Nothing is overwritten and nothing is permanent; boss remove takes it back out. → boss adopt

Several of these going at once

Three side projects of your own, or a room full of other people's. Either way the question stops being how do I build this and becomes which of these is still alive, and how would I know without asking everyone.

What BOSS does: every project writes things down the same way, so you can open any one of them and see where it actually got to — what was decided, what was learned, what's still in the air. There's nothing to keep up to date. It's read straight from the files, so it can't quietly disagree with the work.

Runs per-project on the machine. No account, no server, no telemetry of any kind — nothing to set up and nothing that phones anyone. What BOSS writes down →

Not for two cases, and it'll say so. A codebase that already has a product team around it — you have the structure; this would be a second one arguing with the first. And a weekend throwaway you'll delete on Monday — git init is enough, and anything more is the ceremony BOSS exists to prevent.

▸the snags

The things that go wrong when you build this way

None of these are AI's fault exactly. They're what happens when the building gets fast and nothing else does. BOSS has a specific answer to each — and mostly it stays quiet until one of them is actually happening to you.

"I have twelve ideas and no idea which one is real"

Every idea gets a file with a status, and the board is read from those files. When you've captured several and pressure-tested none, it says so — once — and points at the cheapest way to find out, which is usually a conversation, not a canvas.

"I built the whole thing and still don't know if anyone wants it"

The one that costs the most and gets skipped the most. BOSS designs a fake door — or a wizard-of-oz, or a concierge version — for the riskiest demand assumption, and it runs in days, before the build. Savoia's rule, plainly: make sure you're building the right it before you build it right.

"I keep saying yes and the scope keeps growing"

A short bet-list with a mandatory no-list — because a roadmap that only says yes isn't a decision. It has a fixed appetite, it gets thrown away rather than tended, and each bet's confidence is the evidence grade behind it, so wishful ones look wishful.

"Four things are half-built and none of them are done"

BOSS notices when too much is in flight at once and asks which one is actually moving. It won't reorganise your work — it names the pile, which is usually enough.

"I talked to six people and can't tell what I actually learned"

Paste the transcript — an interview, a sales call, a support thread — and it comes back as honestly-graded records, plus the pains, the jobs and the words people actually used. It also flags the moment you led the witness, which is the part you can't see yourself.

"Someone asked who else does this and I had a vibe, not an answer"

Features, pricing, and an honest "why they might win", kept as a living set you add names to rather than a slide you drew once. Every claim carries a source and the date it was checked, or it's marked unverified — because a confidently invented competitor price is the failure mode here.

"It looks right and I can't tell if it works"

The honest failure mode of building fast. /spec makes you say what "working" means before you build it, /smoke is the gate that answers "is it alive", and /evals is the separate question for anything an AI decides — because alive and correct are not the same test.

"My AI writes a different button on every screen"

Design tokens get scaffolded the moment there's real UI — not at the start, when they'd be ceremony. BOSS watches for styling spread across several files and offers then, so style never gets welded into code one component at a time.

"I don't know what agents to set up, or what to let them touch"

You don't set them up. BOSS ships a roster — builders who make the product, mentors who coach you — each with its tools already declared and scoped. They arrive rung by rung, so you're never handed eleven agents on day one.

"This works at ten users and I suspect it breaks at a thousand"

There's an architecture mentor for exactly this, and its job is to name the load-bearing decision rather than start refactoring — including the answer "not yet, and here's the signal to watch for." Rebuilding early is as expensive as rebuilding late.

"We're both moving fast and quietly drifting apart"

AI makes each of you faster, which left alone pulls a two-person team into parallel. BOSS notices when real work has happened and nothing has been decided together — and it notices through an untouched decision log, never by asking how your teamwork is going, because self-report is exactly what's blind to this.

"I came back after two weeks and lost the thread"

boss status opens with where you are and the one thing to pick up. boss recap reads the week back out of records you already wrote. Neither is a document you maintain, so neither can be out of date.

"We decided this months ago and nobody remembers why"

A decision record with the context, the call, the reasoning — and a falsifier: what would prove this wrong, and by when. It also stamps who decided: you, or you ratifying the AI's suggestion, or the AI on its own. Six months later that distinction turns out to matter a lot.

"The AI bill arrived and I hadn't thought about it"

Declare a budget before the first model call, not after the invoice. /ai-cost wires a per-call ledger, /cost-review reads it back, and if you're charging for the thing, BOSS watches spend against your price rather than against a number you invented.

"I can't afford a security audit and don't know what I'd ask for"

The money path, the destructive path, and the negative path — can user A reach user B's data — plus a secrets scan of the bundle you're about to ship. Needs no AI in your product at all. Agents get their own pass, because tools and memory are a different surface from code. Output is pass or fail with the attack that proved it.

"My AI just read my .env into the chat"

A guard ships with every project that denies reading .env and secrets/ into the model's context. It arrives switched off — it costs a process on every tool call, and BOSS won't spend your latency without asking. One line turns it on when the stakes justify it.

"I don't want to become the thing I used to complain about"

Fake scarcity, the hidden unsubscribe, the confirmshame. One pass probes your shipped product against a catalog of these. A separate check reads what the model proposed — because the dark pattern to worry about now is the one you were never asked about. One audits what you built, the other reads what you were about to be talked into.

And the small ones, which say more about it than the big ones

  • It refuses to make you a one-pager if you have no evidence on file. A handsome page made of assertions is the most convincing wrong thing you can hand someone.
  • It will never message you while you're away. No push, no "while you were out" — and that holds even where the notification would genuinely be useful.
  • Empty sections print as empty. Your weekly recap says "nothing recorded" rather than padding, because a summary that can only go up is a comfort device.
  • It grades your evidence honestly, not generously. "Great idea, I'd use that" lands on the bottom rung and stays there.
  • It won't pick your licence, or assume you're building a company at all. A co-op, a commons and a startup get the same ladder.
  • Every opinion it ships names who it was learned from, when it was last checked, and what would make it wrong.
  • The conscience's only positive signal is silence. If it isn't speaking, nothing it watches is out of shape.
  • It comes back out. boss remove previews first, and everything you wrote stays yours.

▸it working

What it looks like when BOSS actually speaks

Four ideas captured over a couple of weeks, none of them pressure-tested. You open Claude and ask for something unrelated — "add a settings page". Here is exactly what happens, in two halves.

1 · the part that decides whether to speak — real output, no model involved 
a hook reads the project's own files and returns:
  "loop_id": "canvas-loop",  "moment": "caution",  "confidence": "medium"
  "evidence": { entry: 4 captures across 4 files, min 3 — met
                exit:  0 canvases naming a riskiest assumption — not met }

No model was asked "is this person drifting?" A condition was met, in files, and that is checkable. The same project state gives the same answer every time.

2 · the instruction that fires next — and it opens by telling the model to shut up 
If the captures are ONE idea getting sharper — each entry refining the
same bet, narrowing the user, finding the real pain — that's
DEPTH, not avoidance. Stay silent; firing here punishes exactly
the thinking caution should encourage.
Fire only if the captures are scattered … name the *specific* pattern
in one spare line (not a generic "you should validate"), ask what
they'd want to learn, and hand the decision back. Say it at most once.

That is the whole design in one paragraph. The hook decides whether it is allowed to speak; the model only decides how. And the first thing the instruction does is describe the case where speaking would be wrong — because four captures might be one idea getting sharper, and nagging someone for thinking carefully is worse than saying nothing.

3 · here the four captures really were four different products, so it speaks — once 
▸ Four captures, four different products — none of them narrowing
  on who actually has the problem. What would you want to learn
  before building any of them? /canvas sharpens the bet; if you
  already know whose problem it is, /interview preps the call.
 
Then it stops. Your settings page gets built either way.

The first two blocks are copied from a real run — you can reproduce them by putting four captures in a project and typing anything. The third is composed fresh each time, so the words you get will not be these words; that is the half a model does. It did not block the prompt, did not repeat itself, did not grade you, and pointed at a conversation rather than at more BOSS. How it decides →

▸where you are

You should never have to wonder which stop you're at

BOSS grows in four modes, and you unlock them. A one-evening idea gets almost nothing here. A thing with real users gets more. You never pay for ceremony the project hasn't earned, and nothing gets bolted on that you can't take back off.

  1. Quickstart 4 on the team 17 skills
  2. MVP 11 on the team 45 skills
  3. V1 11 on the team 47 skills
  4. Scale 12 on the team 48 skills

You unlock each stop. Nothing is ever removed — the roster only grows.

And every rung names its own entry price before you climb it — then declines to stop you, because it's your project and BOSS isn't your gatekeeper.

climbing a rung you haven't earned yet 
boss unlock mvp

  MVP mode pays for itself when three things are true:
    ✓ an idea captured you can actually build from  (2 on file)
    · at least one real signal on file — something a person actually said or did
        (nothing in docs/evidence/ yet — /interview)
      and you know roughly what the first working version has to do
  The unmarked ones BOSS cannot check — they are yours to judge, not its.
  Missing one? That's fine — but you'll be carrying ceremony you haven't earned.
  Unlocking anyway (BOSS never blocks); the deviation is yours to own.

Two commands answer "where am I" without you keeping anything up to date. boss status opens with you are here and the ladder, then the one thing to pick up next and what you've actually learned so far. boss recap reads the last week back out of your own records — what landed, what you learned, what you decided, what's in flight — and says plainly when a section is empty rather than padding it. ▸the name

Yeah. It's called BOSS. We know.

Everyone's had a bad boss — the one who hovered, took the credit, and never once told you the truth. This is the opposite of that one. BOSS stays quiet until you actually need it. It never puts its name on your work: you ship it, your name's on it. And it won't blow smoke up your skirt — its entire job is telling you when you're fooling yourself.

You're the boss. BOSS just has your back. The name's a joke. The conscience isn't.

don't take our word for it

The claims on this page are checkable.

BOSS asks you to write down what you decided and what would prove it wrong, so it does the same in public. Its decision records are in the repo — 16 of them, including the ones that make it look bad, because they were written before anyone was reading. So is the conscience's own eval suite, so you can run the judgment it will apply to you.

Every practice it ships names who it was learned from, when it was last checked, and what would make it wrong — 33 of them, and 98 outside claims put through the filter, most of which were turned down. How BOSS decides what it believes →

Every byte stays on your machine. The only network call BOSS makes is when you type boss update — a GET to the public npm registry asking for a version number. Nothing is sent about you, your code, or your ideas. No analytics, no telemetry, no phone-home; the check never runs unless you ask for it. It’s ~30 lines in src/update.js and you can read them.

Where this actually is: v0.275.0, built in the open and used daily by its author. Very few other people have used it yet. BOSS is its own first project — it runs its own modes, files its own ideas, and its conscience nudges the person building it. If that sounds like a reason to wait, that’s a fair read.
