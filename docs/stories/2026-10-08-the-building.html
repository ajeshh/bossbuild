The building

Most people building with AI don't have one project. They have six: a real one, two side projects, a client's repo, something from last spring, and a thing they started on Sunday. The hard part isn't building any one of them. It's remembering which is which, where each one stands, and what you meant to do next.

And until now, BOSS came one way: the whole of it. A side project you wanted to keep track of got the same mentors, canvas and customer interviews as a startup. So we drew BOSS as a building. Every project lives on the floor that holds as much BOSS as it wants, and HQ shows all of them at once.

Decided 2026-10-08 (DEC-024, PROG-006). Nothing on this page has shipped yet. The Mailroom is built first.

Which floor do you want?

Lobby

outside

"This one isn't mine, but I still need to keep track of it."

For

A client's repo, your employer's, an open-source project you contribute to.

You get

A card in HQ: what it is, what's next, when you last touched it. Git supplies the rest. Nothing is written into the repo.

Why

You need to find your way back to it, but it isn't yours to change. A tool that drops files into someone else's repo has overstepped.

Why it's enough

The problem here is memory. One line of what and one line of next answer "what was I doing in there?"

Basement

0 · under every floor

You don't choose it. Every floor stands on it.

You get

  • Rules for the AI: what it must never run, and what it asks you before running.
  • A secrets check on every commit, whoever makes it.
  • A .gitignore block that keeps keys and local files out of the repo.

Why

AI writes fast. A key committed once stays in the history and in every clone, even after you delete the line. If you're going to build with AI, set up the right container first.

Why it's enough

These are the mistakes you can't take back. Everything else in BOSS can wait until you want it. This can't.

Mailroom

1

"Hey, track this."

For

The side project you touch once a month. The experiment you might come back to.

You get

The Basement, plus a card in HQ with what this is and what's next.

Why

For most side projects the problem isn't tooling. It's opening the folder after three weeks and not knowing why.

Why it's enough

Two lines and a safe repo. The card keeps you oriented, and nothing else gets in the way of a project that only wants to exist.

Office

2

"Help me hold the context."

For

A project you come back to every week or so, with an AI session open.

You get

  • Working rules the AI reads at the start of every session.
  • A resume of where you left off, read when a session opens and written when it closes.
  • A place for ideas and the decisions you made, with why.

Why

The AI forgets everything between sessions, and so do you. The Office is where the project remembers for both of you.

Why it's enough

For a project you keep returning to, continuity is the whole problem. Here the HQ card updates itself, because every session ends by writing where you are.

Studio

3

"Help me build it. Skip the venture talk."

For

A tool, an open-source project, a gift, a thing for your friends. Anything you want built well that doesn't have to be a business.

You get

  • The building team: a coder, a planner, a tester, a designer, an architecture mentor, a prompt coach.
  • Specs with acceptance criteria, a smoke check, design review, a way to ship it.
  • If it uses AI: evals, red-teaming, cost and failure planning.

Why

Not every project is a business. A Studio project is never asked who pays for it, because nobody has to.

Why it's enough

It has everything about building the thing right, and nothing about whether to build it.

Boardroom

4

"This is a venture. Give me the whole thing."

For

A project that needs users, customers or money to work.

You get

  • A board of mentors: founder, customers, capital, cofounder, and a humane lens that can overrule them.
  • The conscience, which watches for building around your riskiest assumption.
  • The canvas, evidence from real people, interviews, cheap demand tests, money.
  • The four stages: Quickstart, MVP, V1, Scale.

Why

When it's a venture, the riskiest part is usually not the code. It's whether anyone wants it.

Why it's enough

It's all of BOSS.

The door and the elevator

You come in through one door. boss adopt brings in a folder you already have; boss new starts one. Either way you enter at the Basement and ride to the floor you came for. Nothing puts you on the top floor by default.

Later, boss up is the elevator. It shows you what the next floor adds before it adds anything. When BOSS sees a project reaching a threshold, it says so once. Going up is your call.

A floor is a home, not a rank

A side project that lives in the Mailroom forever has done nothing wrong. HQ shows the projects you're working on loudly and lets the rest rest. No red badges, no streaks, no nudging upward. And if a project is done, putting it down is a choice BOSS offers, not a failure it counts.

HQ

HQ is the view across the whole building: every project on this machine, what it is, where it stands, what's next, and which window you have open on it. It reads what each floor already keeps, so it needs nothing new from any project. It's built last, once there's something on every floor to read.
