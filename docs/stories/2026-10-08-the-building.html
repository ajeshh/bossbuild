The building

Most people building with AI don't have one project. They have six: a real one, two side projects, a client's repo, something from last spring, and a thing they started on Sunday. The hard part isn't building any one of them. It's remembering which is which, where each one stands, and what you meant to do next.

And until now, BOSS came one way: the whole of it. A side project you wanted to keep track of got the same mentors, canvas and customer interviews as a startup. So we drew BOSS as a building. Every project signs in at the Lobby, then lives on the floor that holds as much BOSS as it wants. HQ shows all of them at once.

Decided 2026-10-08 (DEC-024, PROG-006). Nothing on this page has shipped yet. The Lobby is built first.

Which floor do you want?

Lobby

0 · reception

"This is dhun. It's a music app. Next, fix the playback bug."

For

Every project. You sign in here on the way to any floor. Some projects stay: a repo that isn't yours, or one you only want to remember.

You get

A card in HQ with the project's name, what it is, and what's next. Git fills in when you last touched it. Nothing is written into the repo.

Why

Most of the forgetting is these three lines. Opening a folder after three weeks, the first question is "what was this, and what was I about to do?"

Why it's enough

For a project you only need to find your way back to, a name, one line of what and one line of next is the whole job. And a repo that belongs to someone else stays untouched.

Security

1 · your badge

Every floor above needs the badge.

You get

  • Rules for the AI: what it must never run, and what it asks you before running.
  • A secrets check on every commit, whoever makes it.
  • A .gitignore block that keeps keys and local files out of the repo.

Why

AI writes fast. A key committed once stays in the history and in every clone, even after you delete the line. If an AI is going to work in the repo, set up the right container first.

Why it's enough

These are the mistakes you can't take back. Everything else in BOSS can wait until you want it. This can't. A project can stop here: safe, and nothing more.

Mailroom

2

"Catch my notes and ideas for this."

For

A project that collects things before it builds them: links, screenshots, half-ideas, a note you jotted at midnight.

You get

  • An inbox: drop in a file, a link or a paste, and it gets kept and sorted.
  • A place for ideas, each with its own record that grows as you add to it.
  • Delivery from HQ: a note you jotted with no project in mind lands here when you assign it.

Why

Ideas arrive at bad times and in the wrong place. The Mailroom gives them one address per project.

Why it's enough

Catching is its own job. A project that's still mostly ideas doesn't need anything more yet.

Office

3

"Remember where I left off."

For

A project you come back to every week or so, with an AI session open.

You get

  • Working rules the AI reads at the start of every session.
  • A resume of where you left off, read when a session opens and written when it closes.
  • A record of the decisions you made, and why.

Why

The AI forgets everything between sessions, and so do you. The Mailroom catches things; the Office remembers them, for both of you.

Why it's enough

For a project you keep returning to, continuity is the whole problem. Here the HQ card updates itself, because every session ends by writing where you are.

Studio

4

"Help me build it. Skip the venture talk."

For

A tool, an open-source project, a gift, a thing for your friends. Anything you want built well that doesn't have to be a business.

You get

  • The building team: a coder, a planner, a tester, a designer, an architecture mentor, a prompt coach.
  • Specs with acceptance criteria, a smoke check, design review, a way to ship it.
  • If it uses AI: evals, red-teaming, cost and failure planning.

Why

Not every project is a business. A Studio project is never asked who pays for it, because nobody has to.

Why it's enough

It has everything about building the thing right, and nothing about whether to build it.

Boardroom

5

"This is a venture. Give me the whole thing."

For

A project that needs users, customers or money to work.

You get

  • A board of mentors: founder, customers, capital, cofounder, and a humane lens that can overrule them.
  • The conscience, which watches for building around your riskiest assumption.
  • The canvas, evidence from real people, interviews, cheap demand tests, money.
  • The four stages: Quickstart, MVP, V1, Scale.

Why

When it's a venture, the riskiest part is usually not the code. It's whether anyone wants it.

Why it's enough

It's all of BOSS.

The door and the elevator

You come in through one door. boss adopt brings in a folder you already have; boss new starts one. Either way you sign in at the Lobby, pass Security, and ride straight to the floor you came for. Nothing puts you on the top floor by default.

BOSS assumes the repo is yours. If git says it belongs to someone else, the door asks before writing anything, and offers to keep the project in the Lobby.

Later, boss up is the elevator. It shows you what the next floor adds before it adds anything. When BOSS sees a project reaching a threshold, it says so once. Going up is your call.

A floor is a home, not a rank

A side project that stays in the Lobby forever has done nothing wrong. HQ shows the projects you're working on loudly and lets the rest rest. No red badges, no streaks, no nudging upward. And if a project is done, putting it down is a choice BOSS offers, not a failure it counts.

HQ

HQ is the view across the whole building: every project on this machine, what it is, where it stands, what's next, and which window you have open on it. It reads what each floor already keeps, so it needs nothing new from any project. It's built last, once there's something on every floor to read.
